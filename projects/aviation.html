How Media Sentiment
Fuels Public Fear of Aviation Disasters

Analyzed whether media framing, not crash frequency, drives public fear of flying. Used NLP sentiment analysis across three lexicons (Bing, AFINN, NRC) on aviation headlines from NewsAPI and the NYT API, cross-referenced against Aviation Safety Network crash records and Google Trends search data from February 2020 to February 2025.

Context

The Fear-Reality Gap

Despite commercial aviation becoming increasingly safer, public fear of flying has grown in the post-pandemic era. This disconnect is likely explained by the availability heuristic: people estimate risk based on how easily examples come to mind. When a crash dominates headlines for days, flying feels dangerous, regardless of the statistics.

This project asks whether media exaggeration, through topic amplification and emotional framing, is the real driver of flight fear, rather than the underlying crash data.

RQ1

Is there a correlation between aviation crash counts and fear-related Google search volume, independent of media coverage?

RQ2

Do more crash headlines lead to more fear-related Google searches?

RQ3

Is media coverage disproportionately high relative to actual crash occurrences?

RQ4

Does negative headline sentiment predict an increase in public fear of planes?

Results

Findings by Research Question

RQ1 — Crashes vs. Fear Searches (No Media)

r = 0.096 p = 0.129 R² = 0.0001

No significant linear relationship between weekly crash counts and "fear of flying" search volume. Crash frequency explains less than 1% of variance in search behavior. Linear regression line is essentially flat.

A Random Forest model revealed a nonlinear threshold: search volume only spikes around 6-8 crashes per week, suggesting fear does not build gradually but activates suddenly at a tipping point. All three models agree that fear spikes in exceptional weeks, not with any consistent pattern tied to raw crash counts.

RQ2 — Headlines vs. Fear Searches

% Var explained: -11.08 Ntree = 500 MSE = 0.407

Random Forest trained on Bing sentiment features (avg_bing, total_bing, n_words, neg_sentiment_ratio, article_count) failed to reliably predict fear-based search spikes. Actual vs. predicted plots show wide dispersion, with the model systematically under-predicting spikes. Article volume alone does not drive public fear. Emotional intensity matters more than count.

RQ3 — Media Volume vs. Actual Crashes

17% of weeks exceeded 5:1 article-to-crash ratio 65% below 2.5:1 median 10% above 7:1

Out of 53 observed weeks, 9 (17%) exceeded a 5:1 article-to-crash ratio, indicating disproportionate coverage. The distribution is right-skewed: most weeks have a reasonable incident-to-coverage ratio, but coverage surges unpredictably during isolated events, driven by factors beyond crash counts.

NLP analysis confirmed the bias in tone: average weekly AFINN score was -0.82 (range: -3.2 to +1.5). The Bing lexicon showed 63% of weeks had net negative sentiment, with the worst week recording 3x more negative than positive words. NRC revealed "fear" (12.3 mentions/week) and "negative" (15.6/week) dominating headlines, more than double the "trust" mentions (4.9/week). Crucially, high crash counts did not always correlate with peak negativity, implying editorial choices drive tone as much as facts.

A binary classification model flagged weeks with an article-to-crash ratio above 2.5:1 (32% of weeks, 17 out of 53). Key predictors: "fear" word count (r = 0.41) and Bing net sentiment (r = -0.38). Weeks with 15+ "fear" mentions were 3x more likely to be flagged. Model F1-score = 0.62, suggesting additional context (event severity, geopolitical factors) would improve classification.

RQ4 — Negative Sentiment as a Predictor of Fear

33% spike at [0.7, 0.8] bin 0-3% in all other bins AFINN: p = 0.939, R² = -0.02

Binning the negative sentiment ratio (0.0 to 1.0) into 10 equal intervals revealed a threshold effect: only the 0.7-0.8 bin produced a 33% rate of fear-related search spikes. All other bins showed 0-3%. Public anxiety does not scale linearly with negativity; it ignites above a critical point of emotional intensity.

AFINN linear regression confirmed no smooth linear relationship (p = 0.939), even in weeks with extreme negativity (e.g., -3.2 AFINN score). Average weekly search interest remained stable (mean = 1.02, SD = 0.71), reinforcing that public attention is event-driven, not sentiment-driven in general.

Central finding: The numbers are not fueling the fear, the narrative is. Fear responds to extreme framing, not to crash counts or headline volume alone. Neither crashes nor coverage drive anxiety unless they cross critical intensity thresholds. Media alarmism mostly misses its mark unless exceptionally emotional.

Limitations & Recommendations

Takeaways

Limitations

  • Free API caps Limited historical data coverage
  • ASN scraping failed Crash data entered manually
  • Lexicon simplicity Word-level analysis misses context
  • Time-lag assumptions Public reaction window is fixed
  • Search as proxy Google Trends is not direct fear measurement
  • Correlation != causation Confounders not fully controlled

Recommendations

  • Regulators Use sentiment monitoring for crisis communication
  • Airlines Proactively address fear spikes in real time
  • Governments Education campaigns to counter sensationalism
  • Media More balanced framing of aviation incidents
